01 The one hard problem

Why any of the rest of this exists

Zooming in makes things bigger. Zoom in far enough and the numbers describing them get bigger too — and a computer's numbers run out. Not gracefully: they get coarse, then wrong, then silent.

bound.less zooms by a factor of 4,096 per level. Five levels down is a magnification of about 1.15 × 1018. If the app simply multiplied coordinates on the way down, a stroke five levels deep would be written at numbers around 7 × 1018, where the smallest difference a computer can still tell apart is over a thousand — so a 40-unit mark collapses to nothing at all. That was measured; it is not a worry.

Three separate failures come from the same source, and each one is a section of this document:

FailureWhat went wrongFixed by
AA magnified copy of a stroke was stored as a many-sided polygon. The next level down blew up one of its sides to fill the screen. The painted edge ended up 263 pixels from where the shape said it was.§7, §9
BWhich region you were "in" depended on how you got there. Aiming one pixel differently on the way down created entirely new regions from three levels deep. Returning to a spot only worked because people steer by what they can see.§5
CDragging a group destroyed its deepest members. At five levels of separation, 82.9% of a deep piece's area was lost — the pieces stayed in register with each other, so the object did not move wrongly, it came apart.§6
The governing rule

Never do arithmetic on a number far larger than the answer you want. Every operation is performed in coordinates sized to the thing being computed — never sized to how far that thing sits from the origin, and never sized to how deep it is.

02 Four ideas

Everything else follows from these

  1. Space is a grid of nested boxes, and a position is an address in it. Not one enormous coordinate system, but a tree of small ones. Where something is, is a list of whole-number steps — one per level — plus a small leftover. Moving something is arithmetic on that list, and its shape is never touched.
  2. Ink is made of circular arcs, and stays arcs. A drawn stroke becomes the exact outline of its painted area — a closed chain of line segments and circular arcs. Enlarging an arc gives an arc; cutting an arc gives an arc. Nothing is approximated by a polygon along the way.
  3. Every object carries its own cutting grid. Ink gets chopped into tiles for storage and display. Those tile lines belong to the object, not to space — so however far the object is moved, the chops land on exactly the same points of it.
  4. Never take a shortcut across levels. Every transform is applied one level at a time. Combining five levels into a single multiplication looks equivalent and is not: it destroys the small differences that carry the detail.
Two words, kept strictly apart

A frame is one box of the fixed grid of space. It never moves; it decides which objects are looked at. A tile is where an object is cut. They are the same size — 131,072 units — but they are different grids, and a frame is never permitted to cut anything.

Confusing the two is the most expensive mistake available in this codebase. It has been made twice and caught twice, and §8 is what it costs.

05 Space: a grid of nested boxes

Where you are is a division, not a memory

Principle P4

Which frame you are in is a function of where you are, not of how you got there.

Space is divided, at every level, into square boxes 131,072 units across. Each contains 4,096 × 4,096 child boxes, each 32 of the parent's units wide. A frame is one of these boxes.

In the codedoc corrected

The design's §5.1 gives a box the extent [i·G, (i+1)·G) — cornered on its index. That is wrong and was corrected during the build.

The reason is in the diagram opposite: cornering puts the world origin on a box corner, so zooming into it walks to the corner of every box at every depth, and every cut splits four ways. Measured while building the first version — a hole cut at the view centre straddled four tiles at six consecutive depths.

Tiles are centred on their index too, for the same reason one level down.

frameLattice.cellOf — a round, not a floor

The edge between a parent and a child

Every box knows one thing about its parent: how to convert a coordinate. It is always the same shape — subtract the child box's centre, then multiply by 4,096. The centre is a whole number and the multiplier is a power of two, so the conversion is exact in both directions.

Subtracting before scaling is not cosmetic. Doing it the other way forms a huge product and then adds a huge offset of the opposite sign, which throws away the small digits before the addition can recover them.

Four things that fall out of a fixed grid

  • Returning is a lookup. Same place, same box, always — failure B has nothing left to fail at.
  • Neighbours are countable. The box next door is index ± 1; when that runs off the end of the range it carries up to the parent, exactly as a long addition carries.
  • Boxes cannot overlap. An object is never ambiguously in two of them.
  • Whole mechanisms were deleted, not tuned. The reuse radius, the nearest-frame search and sibling-spawning are gone.

The two invariants

Invariant 1

A screen is never wider than a box. Holds unaided up to a 4,096-pixel canvas. A convenience, not a requirement.

Invariant 2 — load-bearing

An object never reaches past its box's immediate neighbours. This is what makes "what else could overlap here?" answerable by checking eight boxes instead of searching.

Invariant 2 is enforced two ways. A stroke drawn too wide is promoted to the level above at pen-up, dividing every coordinate by 4,096 — a change of units, not a loss of accuracy. And an object dragged out of its box is re-homed into the box it now sits in when the drag ends.

Crossing, and stepping sideways

The camera keeps its in-level zoom inside the band from 1/32 to 256. Past the top it crosses down into the child box the view centre falls in; past the bottom it crosses up into its parent. A box is only about three screens across, so panning can also walk you clean out of one — the camera then shifts sideways into the neighbour, which is pure bookkeeping and exact, because the offset is a whole number of boxes.

A sideways shift never fires while the pen is down. A live stroke's samples are being written against its box's origin; moving the box mid-gesture would append later points against a different origin and tear the stroke in half. It waits, and the over-wide stroke is promoted at pen-up.

The uncle

In the codeadded

The uncle is not in the design at all. Its §5.4 treats coarser off-branch content as a curiosity that "cannot arise in a spine".

It arises constantly, and it was found by an erase test that cut a hole and then could not find the object. The pickup of eight neighbouring boxes at every step of the chain was added afterwards and measured at no cost.

TileStore._ringNatives

Because a box is only three screens wide, zooming in slightly off-centre puts you in the box next door — and ink that is plainly on screen is suddenly a sibling of your ancestor: coarser than you, but not above you. The magnify chain skipped it and the minify path skipped it too, so it vanished. The fix is to pick up the eight neighbouring boxes' objects at every step of the chain. Invariant 2 is what makes that complete, and neighbouring origins differ by whole boxes, so the translation costs nothing in accuracy.

06 Position is an address

A move is arithmetic on a list of whole numbers

Principle P5

Position is an address, not a coordinate: a path of integer box indices plus one small local offset. A move is integer arithmetic on that path.

The path is not stored on the object; it is the chain of boxes it sits in, shared with everything else in them. The object holds a pointer to its box and its own local coordinates.

In the codedoc superseded

The erase document's §5.1 specifies something else entirely: position as a sparse map of per-level offsets, applied during the descent, with residue bookkeeping to make a slow drag match a fast one.

None of that was built. The lattice made it unnecessary — exact integer arithmetic gives path-independence for free — and that document's §5.4 residue tracking was deleted rather than ported.

That section still reads as current. It is the single most misleading page in the design records.

LevelMap.displaceFrame · Document.rehomeById

Five consequences

  • Deep members are not translated at all. They are addressed relative to their ancestor, so adjusting the shared ancestor's digit moves them exactly, with no arithmetic on their geometry. Failure C is not mitigated; it is unreachable.
  • A slow drag lands exactly where a fast one does. Each step is recomputed from where the drag started, not from the last event, so forty one-pixel steps and one forty-pixel flick are literally the same arithmetic on the same numbers.
  • Drag-and-return is bit-exact, and so is undo — which restores the geometry the drag started from rather than translating by the negative.
  • An address may extend below the object's own level. Moving something nine million units at level 5 is a real, visible one-unit move at level 3, arrived at by accumulation. A local coordinate holds about three levels of this before its own precision gives out — which is also where the digits stop being meaningful, and the two bounds agreeing is the reassuring part.
  • Selection becomes a tree query. A box wholly inside the lasso contributes its entire subtree with no per-object test; wholly outside, it and everything under it is skipped.

A drag with the camera above the object works the other way and is safe by the opposite argument: the displacement measured in that object's units is the drag divided by 4,096 per level of separation, and shrinking a number cannot make it explode.

07 Ink is arcs

From pen samples to an exact outline

Decision D1

A piece is its two endpoints and its bulge. Centre and radius are derived, never carried — they are redundant, and they are the only part that can explode.

In the codenot built

A piece still carries {line, C, r, a0, sweep, A, B} — centre and radius included, in memory and in the save file. This is the last decision from the design's locked list still outstanding, and it is acknowledged as such.

What holds the guarantee up is narrower than the design intends. The straightness test is bulge-only, so no radius can spoil a freeze. But finding where a curve meets a grid line reads the stored centre directly: u = (k − C[axis]) / r.

That is safe only structurally — a line is never chopped, and by the third crossing everything is a line, so a curve is only ever chopped at shallow depths where its centre is an ordinary number. A structural argument standing in for a representation change.

arcPerimeter.subPiece · freeze.arcCuts:96

A drawn stroke is not stored as a line with a width. At pen-up it resolves into the exact outline of the area it paints: one closed chain of straight segments and circular arcs. That outline is the object from then on; the centreline is dropped.

Why arcs, and not curves

The reason is the offset. Widening a stroke means finding the curve that runs a fixed distance either side of its centreline. For a general curve that offset is not a curve of the same kind — it has to be approximated, and then every question asked afterwards (where do the two edges cross? which bits are buried under the pen?) is asked of an approximation while burial is judged against the exact centreline. The two disagree, and at a shallow crossing the disagreement is amplified enormously. That was a whole family of bugs.

The offset of a circular arc is a circular arc — same centre, radius plus or minus the pen. Exact, and drawable directly by both the browser's vector formats. Two arcs cross where two circles cross: one square root, no iteration, and no chance of finding one crossing twice at two slightly different places.

One arc cannot replace one curve segment — an arc through two points with the direction fixed at both ends is over-determined. Two arcs meeting smoothly have exactly the freedom needed, so the pen builds pairs, and the smoothness the user already sees is unchanged.

Resolving a stroke — eight phases, all interruptible

Build both edges plus the two end caps as one closed chain, cut it wherever it crosses itself, throw away the fragments buried under the pen, stitch the survivors into closed loops. Every step is exact except the last judgement, which is a measurement by nature.

1 centrelineThe pen's arc chain — already built while drawing, one sample at a time
2 chainBoth edges and both caps as one closed ring
3 oracleIndex the centreline for distance queries
4 pre-cullDrop pieces wholly inside the pen
5 cutSplit at every self-crossing
6 classifyKeep the fragments not buried
7 stitchWalk survivors into closed loops
8 finishDrop hairlines, fix handedness, check the joins

Three details carry real weight:

  • The edge point at a shared corner is computed once and given to both neighbours. The chain is watertight because it was built that way, not because the arithmetic happened to agree.
  • Consecutive pieces are never tested against each other. They share a point and a direction, which makes their circles touch — and two touching circles meet at exactly the one point they already share. An immediate neighbour has nothing to contribute and everything to confuse.
  • Every phase can stop mid-item and resume. Deferring between whole objects is not enough: one heavy stroke was an indivisible multi-second job with no point inside it where control came back. Each burst re-tunes towards a millisecond, and nothing is published until the whole thing is done — so a half-resolved outline can never be drawn, stored, or cut against.

What if it does not close?

An outline that fails to close is not a shape: it paints nothing, its inside/outside test means nothing, and the save format refuses the whole drawing over one of them. So a failed resolve is sealed — the broken ends chained together on the cheapest joins available and closed with straight lines, the resulting slivers dropped. It is an honest closed shape rather than a pretence the geometry was right, and how much edge was fabricated is recorded, because a seal a fifth of the pen wide is invisible while one at 45% of the loop is a straight line drawn across the middle of a stroke.

08 The object's own grid

Where ink gets chopped, and where a curve becomes a line

Ink cannot be carried down whole — a level-0 object shown at level 5 is 1018 times its own size. So at each crossing it is chopped into tile-sized pieces and only the relevant pieces continue. Chopping raises a question with a surprising amount riding on it: whose grid?

Decision D4 · §6.6

Frames belong to space; tiles belong to the object. Same size, different grids. Freeze only on the object's own tile boundaries, never on a frame clip.

The freeze

Decisions D2 · D3

Keep the freeze, for cheapness — the arc will get comically straight anyway. And freeze at the same time every time you bake it: determinism, not lateness, is the requirement.

Once a piece has been chopped, the engine asks: does it still bulge more than a quarter of a pixel? If not, it is replaced by the straight line between its own two endpoints — and from that moment the line is the truth. Nothing can consult what it replaced; at that depth the replaced arc's centre is not even a computable number.

Three rules that make the freeze safe
  1. It is measured on the piece visible at this level, never on a sub-piece. The old code flattened a curve into eight hundred tiny chords at once. Each was within a quarter pixel of the span it was fitted to — and then the next level magnified one of them to fill the screen.
  2. The replacement is the chord between the piece's own endpoints — never a best fit. Both endpoints are unchanged to the last bit, so a neighbouring piece that has not frozen still meets them exactly.
  3. The decision can never un-happen. One level deeper you see a smaller portion of the same curve, so it bulges less. Nothing needs storing, and no user ever sees a line turn back into a curve.

In practice a normal pen stroke first freezes at the third crossing and is entirely straight lines by the fourth.

In the codeprinciple violated

The chop and the freeze happen in exactly one place — the render chain. Neither runs on the erase path.

When an erase descends to cut ink that lives at a coarser level, the ink is carried down by a plain projection and clip. It arrives as unfrozen arcs. So at the depth where you are looking at a straight line, the cut is computed against the curve that line replaced.

The design's own §4.3 says the opposite in as many words: after the freeze there is no true arc — nothing in the system can consult it. The build note that added this calls it deliberate, on the grounds that what is stored should stay exact. Both cannot be right.

It is also how a 6.5 × 1010 radius reached a routine that had to intersect it with a radius of 21 — the arithmetic failure of §11.

The seam, and the two boundaries

Neighbouring painted pieces have to overlap by about a pixel, or anti-aliasing leaves a visible hairline down every boundary — composite two half-covered opaque fills and a quarter of the background still shows through. So the clip rect is the tile grown by the seam pad, still a property of the object and the tile, and therefore identical in every cache square that holds it.

But the overhang's own ends are not the object's edge — they are a duplicate of what the next tile holds properly, cut short. If the level below measured one for straightness it would freeze it to a chord that tile disagrees with. So the clip marks the ends it made, those marks survive every transform, and a piece carrying one stays a curve.

09 Painting a view

Three kinds of content, one z-order

Going up: the chain

A tile is built from its parent's tile. That is why failure A was so damaging — whatever a tile held was inherited by everything below it. The chain now carries exact arcs: clipping an arc against a rectangle gives an exact sub-arc, and enlarging an arc gives an arc, so the chain stays exact however deep it goes. Nothing along it is ever frozen to a line; that happens once, at paint time, against nothing that is stored.

Two shortcuts keep the cost bounded. A coarse object that floods a whole tile with no edge in it is replaced by a four-cornered rectangle, so geometry can never outgrow a tile. And a shape lying wholly inside a tile has nothing to clip and passes straight through — the common case for anything shown from a finer level, worth 5.5 ms per tile per member down to 0.2.

In the codediffers

The minify path still flattens to polygons. The design's fix for failure A is stated for the chain in general; only the magnify half of it was built.

It is safe for exactly one structural reason: a minified tile is a leaf. The chain reads the parent's magnified tiles and its objects, never its minified ones, so a polygon made here can never be inherited or used as an operand.

This is safety by arrangement, not by construction. Feed a minified tile into a derivation and failure A comes straight back.

TileStore._downPieces → shapeRingsInRect

Going down: direct projection

Finer ink is shrunk in a single jump, safe at any distance because the coordinates only get smaller. Content is measured at the level's deepest zoom, so the size verdict is view-independent and does not flicker as the camera moves.

Drawing it

  • One group per object, sorted by id. Ids are handed out in creation order, so id is drawing order — a newer object always draws over an older one regardless of which of the three classes it arrived by.
  • Rebuild only what changed. Each group carries a signature of its pieces; a pure camera move touches nothing but one transform.
  • A family paints as one path. When an erase has split an object across levels its pieces meet exactly, with no overlap — and two separately-composited shapes meeting like that leave up to a quarter of the background showing. Drawn as one path, coverage accumulates before anything composites.
  • Translucent ink is grouped, then faded. Pieces are drawn at full strength inside a group whose opacity is the object's, so overlapping pieces union rather than double-darkening.
  • Two workarounds for browser limits, both measured rather than guessed: paths are written relative to a per-scene local origin, re-anchored when the view drifts too far; and an object whose features are very small in the numbers handed over — however much the transform then enlarges them — is rescaled by a power of two with its own transform divided by the same, which is pixel-identical by construction.

10 Erasing

The hardest thing in the app, and the rule that makes it simple

The rule · erase §2

An erase cuts the representation at the level it was made in. At an object's own level that representation is the object, so it is cut in place. Anywhere else it is a tile, so the cut is made there. One rule, two outcomes — no depth threshold to tune.

Why this is necessary: zooming 4,096× per level makes "the object is far bigger than the eraser" the normal case. Four levels down, the eraser's radius is about 3 × 10−17 of the object's own extent — an order of magnitude below what a computer can distinguish at all. The hole's coordinates and the object's are the same number. No change of grid, range or origin reaches this.

In the codedoc reversed

The erase document's §4.1 specifies the cut as a recipe — the object carries the eraser's footprint and every tile bake re-subtracts it at its own fidelity. That was reversed by decision in 2026-08-05: pixel level precision is fine, just start baking as soon as the eraser stroke is done.

The section is marked superseded in place, but the module that implemented it is still in the tree with its header intact, and the smaller claims around it — §4.2's stored per-object clip, §8.1's cut frozen at one fidelity — no longer describe anything.

geometry/erase.js — dead

What happens when you drag the eraser

  1. It commits instantly as a white stroke. Drawing order alone makes the picture correct — it covers exactly what was below it when drawn — so the gesture costs nothing however complex the drawing is.
  2. The real cut happens in the background, one object at a time, in eight-millisecond slices, staying out of the way while the pointer or camera is busy but never for more than a second and a half in total.
  3. When every object beneath has been handled, the white stroke is silently removed.
  4. One gesture is one undo, however many objects it touched and however many levels it descended.

Cutting below an object's home level

In the codediffers

The design describes the cede as a float guillotine — subtract a rectangle from a bounding box, take the four leftover rectangles, group them into connected chunks by testing which share an edge.

None of that survives. The parent is cut by the same exact arc boolean everything else uses, and connected pieces come out of it directly. The special case existed only to dodge the integer library's grid, and with no grid anywhere there is nothing left for it to protect against.

The three subtle bugs the design spends a page on — a cell is not a chunk, a ring is not a chunk, abutting paths do not merge — are all bugs of the guillotine and cannot occur now.

Document.cedeTileById → subtractShape

Two properties are worth stating explicitly.

The parent is cut, not annotated. The tile is physically removed from the parent's outline. It used to keep its geometry whole and merely record a rectangle it had given away, with rendering subtracting the hole per view — which meant a stale picture on the fast zoom path, 232–386 ms renders rebuilding it, and a parent still whole underneath, so deciding whether the object had come apart needed a second, worse cut.

A cede is precision-safe where the erase itself is not. The objection that produced the old model was true of the erase — thousands of times finer than the parent's own units — and false of the tile, which is 32 parent units and perfectly ordinary to represent.

Coming apart

A ceded tile is the one and only place two levels of one object meet: the parent paints everything outside the rectangle, the child everything inside, and they share exactly its boundary. So "is this still one object?" is answered by walking that boundary and asking whether the parent's ink and the child's ink touch along the same stretch of it — measured in a normalised parameter running 0 to 4 around the perimeter, so the parent's units and the child's 4,096-times-larger units give the same number and no transform is ever composed across the crossing.

In the codediffers

Two changes to severance the design does not anticipate.

It stopped being a cut. §3.2 says the parent splits into two objects; the implementation looked for an axis to clip down. Because ceding already leaves the parent in connected pieces, severance is now pure re-labelling — no geometry is touched at all. The old version also declined on any cut that was not dead straight, which is what a real scribbled erase always looks like, so it never fired in use.

It asks about geometry, not ancestry. Once re-homing settles each member on its own coordinates, two members of one object can sit in the same place on different branches — and the object was reported as three objects without changing at all.

KobinEngine._resplitFamily · _familyComponents

Every native in the family is a node; those meetings are the edges; the object is severed exactly when the graph falls apart.

A deliberate bias, stated once and applied everywhere

Every judgement about connectivity rounds towards still whole. Corners count on both sides; a bare touch counts as contact; a doorway the walk did not come through is treated as a bridge. The asymmetry is the reason: an object wrongly left whole renders correctly and merely drags as one lump, while an object wrongly severed comes apart under the user's hands and cannot be put back.

The scenario this is built for: erase 98% through something, zoom in, erase 98% of what remains, and keep going until it finally parts. The neck shrinks about 50× per round while the zoom grows 4,096×, so it converges fast — and since a ceded tile is always 32 of its own level's units while the neck inside it shrinks by 4,096 per crossing, once the cut fits inside one tile every ancestor above sees a doorway that spans its neck. That is why the verdict reaches the top.

Erasing from further out

An erase made above ceded territory walks down the chain it overlaps: anything it fully covers is deleted outright, subtree and all; anything it partly covers is recursed into. Cheap by construction, because the eraser is enormous at those depths. The point is that erasing means the same thing at every zoom.

11 The exact cut

Subtracting one shape from another with no polygon library anywhere

Every previous version of this went through an integer-based polygon library, which rounds every coordinate onto a grid whose step is set by the largest number in play. In an app that magnifies 4,096× per level that is the defect rather than a detail: a hole three levels below its object is finer than one grid step and rounds away entirely.

There is no grid here. Both shapes are chains of arcs, and:

straightenAny arc bulging less than its own numbers can express becomes a line
pass throughLoops whose boxes miss the other shape are settled with no geometry at all
cutSplit both shapes wherever they cross, and wherever they lie on top of each other
classifyEach fragment kept or dropped by asking whether its midpoint is inside the other shape
stitchPair the loose ends at each junction by angle, then walk the pairs into loops
closeDrop hairlines; seal anything the walk could not finish, and count it

The pass-through step is what keeps the cost proportional to the gesture rather than to the drawing: an eraser touches one part of one stroke, and on a real document nearly every loop is settled without any geometry at all.

The five things that were hard

ProblemResolution
Whose scale?"Are these two points the same point?" needs a radius. Taken from the bigger shape it swallows the smaller one whole — and an eraser a millionth of its target's size is the normal case. It is taken from the smaller shape, and from the coordinates where the two actually meet, not from the largest number in the room. Getting this wrong once produced an object of 871 pieces in 68 unclosed chains that painted nothing and made a whole drawing unsaveable.
Same radius, twiceThe slack deciding "did this crossing land on that piece" must be the same radius that decides whether two points are one point. Otherwise a crossing within welding distance is dropped for both sides — one way in with no way out, so the fragments cannot pair and the walk hands back chains that never close.
Shapes that touch along an edgeTwo boundaries lying exactly on top of each other have no crossing to find, but the overlap still has to start and end somewhere. Those ends are cut explicitly, and the shared stretch is decided by which way each boundary travels — the interior is always on the left, so same direction means one covers the other, opposite means they are back to back.
Huge radius, small radiusInk carried two levels down arrives as arcs of radius 6.5 × 1010 — nearly straight, so the radius is enormous — and gets cut by an eraser of radius 21. The textbook circle-circle formula squares both radii, and the noise in the big square is two thousand times larger than the whole answer being sought. Crossings vanished. The formula is rewritten so no expression ever holds the big radius squared while wanting an answer the size of the small one.
A walk that gives upA configuration landing exactly on a discrete decision — an eraser running tangent to the edge it cuts, three arcs meeting at one point — can pair ends into a chain with no way home. The boolean retries once at a different welding radius. If it still fails, the chain is closed with a straight line and counted, because losing a real stretch of boundary is worse than an edge that is straight where it should curve.
In the codeconsequence

Row four is where §8's missing freeze surfaces as a real defect rather than an argument.

A radius of 6.5 × 1010 only reaches the boolean because the erase path carries unfrozen arcs. Had the ink been chopped and frozen the way the render chain does it, those pieces would have been straight lines, and a line-against-arc intersection has none of this problem.

The arithmetic is now correct either way, which is the right fix at that level. But it is a repair underneath a design violation, not instead of one.

12 Identity and selection

What counts as one thing

An object's id is its creation number, which is also its place in the drawing order and never changes. On top of that sits one extra idea: a family key. Pieces of an object that live at several levels — the parent's remnant and everything it ceded — share one key, so they select, move and undo together, while a fragment the eraser cut fully loose gets no key and becomes an ordinary independent object.

Two rules about the key exist because both were violated and both cost real debugging:

  • Turning a stroke into its resolved outline keeps the id. It used to mint a new one, so any reference taken beforehand silently named nothing afterwards.
  • A cut leaving one region keeps the key: that is the same object with less ink. Several regions is a genuine split, and the pieces are rightly strangers.

The two ways to select

GestureBehaviour
clickSelects the topmost thing under the cursor. This is how you reach something at depth, where a coarse object covers the whole canvas and no loop can bound anything.
drag on paperDraws a lasso. Everything fully enclosed is selected, including objects far too small to see — a sub-pixel object has a position and an extent whether or not the screen can show them.
drag on inkMoves whatever is selected. Pressing an already-selected object keeps the whole selection; pressing a different one selects it alone.
ctrlAdds or removes. A ctrl-lasso overlapping the selection adds; one drawn purely inside it removes.

The lasso is deliberately asymmetric, and both directions are correct. From far above, tiny things are selectable. From far below a lasso selects nothing — everything reachable down there is coarse ink magnified thousands of times, so no loop drawn on a screen can bound any of it. That is what clicking is for.

In the codediffers

The design says a fully-enclosed object is selected. The code tests the object's upright bounding rectangle.

A diagonal or curved stroke lying comfortably inside the loop is skipped whenever its bounding corners poke out. Correct by the letter of the rule and a miss to the person drawing the loop — and the first thing to rule out on the open report that the lasso sometimes misses things.

KobinEngine._rectInActive → rectInsidePolygon

The search walks outward from the current box rather than down from the root, because from the root the scale factor would be 4,096depth before the first useful comparison. Every box gets one of three answers: out of reach (skip it and everything under it), enclosed (take the whole subtree with no per-object test), or straddling (ask its own index and recurse). What makes the pruning sound is invariant 2. Measured on a six-level tower of twelve objects, a loop round the whole canvas tests two of them and a loop in an empty corner tests none — and both give the identical answer to asking all twelve.

13 Saving

One JSON document, validated at the door in both directions

A drawing file holds four things: metadata, where the camera was, the tree of boxes, and the objects grouped by which box each is homed in.

An outline is written as a flat list of numbers. Consecutive pieces share an endpoint exactly — the invariant the whole representation rests on — so only the loop's start point and each piece's far end are stored. Writing both ends would double the file and invite a reload where the two no longer match. A resolved outline is usually smaller than the samples it came from: a captured 4,962-sample stroke resolves to 2,195 pieces, and a dense scribble shrinks by an order of magnitude.

The object's chopping offset is saved with it, because it is geometry: drop it and a reloaded drawing subdivides somewhere else, which moves every straightened chord in it.

Decision D8

No migration. A file in the old format is refused with a clear error, never converted — converting means rewriting stored coordinates, the one operation this design exists to avoid.

Two further policies:

  • Damage is repaired on the way in, not refused. The format validates that every outline closes, and a build once produced ones that did not. Refusing those means refusing to open a drawing over an object that can be mended, so they are stitched and sealed at load and the drawing opens, renders and saves.
  • Retired fields are stripped at the door rather than carried as inert state that still looks meaningful. A field recording where a hole should have been is worse than no field at all.
In the codeopen defect

Local autosave fails silently when browser storage fills. The drawing then exists only in the open tab, with no warning that it is not being saved.

This is not a design divergence — the design does not cover it — but it is the one gap in this section that has already cost a real drawing.

hooks/useKobinEngine.js

Locally, drawings autosave into per-canvas browser storage. In the cloud they are compressed and split into 700 KiB chunks under a parent record, committed in groups — and the parent is written last, because readers key off it, so an interrupted save never looks complete. Metadata stays on the parent so listing the gallery never downloads a drawing.

14 Scenes and the scale bar

Two features that inherit the same constraint

Scenes

A drawing with no bottom needs a way back to the places worth returning to. Scenes are discovered automatically by clustering ink, and they obey the engine's own discipline: no global frame exists, so every calculation projects through at most two adjacent levels.

The clever part is the unit. There are no physical screens in the maths — all distances are measured relative to stroke width, which already encodes the zoom the ink was drawn at and is identical on every device. So the same drawing yields the same scenes on a phone and a desktop. A stroke's "screen" is a square 600 times its own width; ink joins across gaps up to 1.5 of those.

There is no minimum size and no minimum stroke count. The tiniest dot drawn three thousand times deeper than everything else is a scene — that is the entire point of the feature. Much-finer ink inside a scene becomes a nested scene, recursively, with one guard: a nested scene must be meaningfully smaller than its parent, or it is just the parent's view again.

The scale bar

The bar tells you how big what you are looking at actually is, across a range from the astronomical to below an atom. It is a rule-based engine rather than a formula: units are organised into ladders, each candidate reading is tested against the preference range in force, and the resolution is the lowest in-range number that qualifies. The user can drag to set a scale, and the reading follows that choice until the zoom leaves the band it was set in.

15 How the engine watches itself

Because "something was slow" invites a guess about what

Four instruments run at once, and they exist because a timer inside the engine reported 11 ms while the browser reported a 1.5-second frame. That gap cost four wrong diagnoses.

InstrumentWhat it records
operation logEvery operation past an 8 ms floor, with what it was working on. High-frequency operations are aggregated rather than logged individually, or one pinch would evict everything else. A trace mode removes the floor — which is exactly what once hid a session with 62 seconds elapsed and 157 ms recorded.
long framesStraight from the browser: how much of a slow frame was script, which functions, how much was forced layout, and what was left over for painting. That last number is the one no timer inside the engine can ever see.
the journalWhat the user did, not just what the document ended up as. Which erase, where, at what zoom, what it cut, whether the object came apart — and, critically, why anything was refused. A refusal is otherwise invisible: the eraser is marked done, the mark is consumed, and the ink was never cut. That is exactly what "I erase and then it just disappears" looks like.
growth logWhat accumulates over a session — object counts, cached tiles, empty buckets. One measured session had 4,343 empty buckets out of 4,369; dropping them took a render from 195 ms to 23.

All of it can be posted as a single report containing the camera, the counts, the timings, the journal, and a loadable snapshot of the drawing — which is how a bug reported in a browser becomes a failing test offline.
